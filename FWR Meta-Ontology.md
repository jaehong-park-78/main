1. Introduction
Traditional ontology has tended to regard existence as a fixed entity. This paper argues that existence should instead be understood as a relational and temporal process of emergence. Existence is not a self-contained object but an event continuously constituted through interaction and transition.
This study proposes the following core proposition:
Existence is a dynamic event emerging from the interaction of Flow, Wave, and Resonance.
This proposition avoids reduction to a single ontological cause while providing a unified framework to explain the generation, dissolution, stability, and collapse of existence through dynamical structure.
Scope. FWR is applicable to systems that admit a graph representation of relational structure. For non-network domains, a graph representation must be constructed before FWR variables can be operationalized; such representation is not always possible, and the framework does not claim universal applicability.
2. Dynamical Formulation of Existence
2.1 Existence Equation
Existence cannot be reduced to a single ontological term. It emerges as a composite phenomenon of flow, phase, and relation. No single component is sufficient: the suppression of any one variable to zero annihilates E(t) regardless of the magnitude of the others.
2.2 Sign Interpretation
The term \sin(\varphi_W(t)) ranges over [-1, 1]. The sign carries ontological content:
E(t) > 0: Constructive regime. Flow, phase, and resonance are mutually reinforcing. Relational structure is forming or consolidating.
E(t) < 0: Annihilative regime. Phase is inverted relative to the relational structure. Existence undergoes net withdrawal or dissolution. The magnitude |E(t)| still quantifies the intensity of this process.
E(t) = 0: A transition point, or the collapse of at least one component.
This interpretation is consistent with the physics of wave interference, where phase inversion produces destructive superposition. In FWR, destructive phase corresponds not to non-existence but to a mode of existence characterized by relational contraction.
2.3 Component Definitions
(1) Flow
S(t) denotes the system state; F(t) is the magnitude of its rate of change. Flow captures dynamism, transformability, and energetic drive. F(t) \geq 0 always.
(2) Wave
W(t) is the instantaneous wave intensity; \varphi_W(t) is the accumulated phase. Wave influences existence through phase structure rather than amplitude alone.
Initial phase \varphi_0: \varphi_0 is a deterministic initial condition specific to each system, analogous to the initial position in classical mechanics. It is not treated as a random variable. Each system carries its own \varphi_0, which must be specified or estimated from the system's state at t = 0. Ensemble averaging over \varphi_0 is not prescribed by FWR; doing so would yield \langle \sin(\varphi_W) \rangle \to 0 under uniform phase distributions, collapsing \langle E(t) \rangle \to 0 — an ontologically vacuous result inconsistent with the framework's purpose.
(3) Resonance
R(t) is a dimensionless order parameter satisfying 0 \leq R(t) \leq 1, representing relational connectivity density. Resonance is an emergent property of relationships within the system, not an intrinsic property of isolated entities.
Concrete specifications (domain-dependent):
Network-based: R(t) = \dfrac{1}{N(N-1)} \displaystyle\sum_{i \neq j} A_{ij}(t)
Information-based: R(t) = \dfrac{I_{\text{shared}}(t)}{I_{\text{total}}(t)}
3. Crisis Axiom and Dark Phase
3.1 Crisis Condition
A(t) denotes external perturbation intensity, defined operationally as the rate of node removal or connection loss per unit time within the relational network. \tau is the critical threshold below which the system maintains normal resonance.
3.2 Piecewise Resonance Model
This describes power-law decay of resonance density beyond the critical threshold. The function is continuous at A = \tau: both branches yield R_0 at the boundary.
Discontinuity of dR/dA at A = \tau: The left derivative is 0; the right derivative is -\gamma R_0 / \tau. This first-order discontinuity is not a defect — it reflects a genuine phase transition, analogous to a kink in a free energy curve at a critical point. The system's sensitivity to perturbation changes discontinuously at \tau.
The exponent \gamma > 0 is a free parameter requiring domain-specific calibration. It is not fixed a priori. For reference, \gamma \approx 2.5 in 2D lattice percolation (Stauffer & Aharony, 1994), but FWR makes no assumption that relational networks share 2D lattice topology.
3.3 Full Existence Equation
Dimensional consistency is preserved: R(t) remains dimensionless throughout.
3.4 Dark Phase
The Dark Phase (A(t) \gg \tau, R(t) \to 0) is characterized by:
Collapse of resonance networks
F(t) may remain nonzero or increase — internal dynamics persist
Metastable pre-reconfiguration state
The Dark Phase is not mere destruction but a preparatory regime, analogous to latent heat regions in thermodynamic phase transitions: the system absorbs perturbation without immediate structural collapse, retaining conditions for new relational formation.
4. Relational Accumulation T(t)
4.1 Definition
T(t) is the time-integrated relational connectivity density. It is a structural variable, not an epistemological one. It captures the historical depth of a system's relational configuration — the structural memory that E(t) alone cannot express.
4.2 Relationship to E(t)
E(t): instantaneous existence — how a system exists at moment t
T(t): accumulated relational structure — why a system persists, and how deep its relational history is
A system with high T(t) has sustained dense relational structure over time and is structurally more resistant to perturbation. A system with low T(t), regardless of its instantaneous E(t), has shallow structural roots.
During crisis, R(t) decreases, suppressing dT/dt. The rate of accumulation falls. T(t) never decreases — accumulated history is not erased — but its growth rate is arrested. This captures structural weakening without annihilating relational memory.
4.3 Exponentially Weighted Variant
The decay constant \lambda \geq 0 is domain-specific. \lambda = 0 is the limiting case of perfect memory and recovers the base definition T_0(t) = T(t). For \lambda > 0, distant relational history is discounted, modeling systems where past connections decay in influence over time.

6. Stability and Transition Analysis
High-R states provide structural stability but reduced adaptability. Low-R states allow flexibility but lack structural coherence. Intermediate states maximize sensitivity to perturbation and capacity for reorganization.
Existence is thus redefined as a problem of dynamic coordination among stability, complexity, and adaptability — not static optimization of any single variable. The multiplicative structure of E(t) encodes this: no component dominates; all three must be sustained.
7. Falsification Conditions
FWR advances one primary falsifiable prediction:
Prediction (Differential Recovery Hypothesis):
Two systems sharing identical instantaneous values of F(t), \varphi_W(t), and R(t) at the onset of crisis will exhibit different post-crisis recovery trajectories if and only if their T(t) values differ. The system with higher T(t) will show shallower resonance collapse or faster relational recovery.
Rationale: T(t) encodes the structural redundancy and alternative pathway density accumulated over the system's relational history. Higher T(t) implies more bypass routes available during perturbation, independent of the instantaneous connectivity R(t).
Proposed simulation design:
Generate two networks with identical topology (e.g., Barabási–Albert, N = 1000, m = 3).
Grow Network A slowly over t = 1000 steps; grow Network B rapidly over t = 100 steps. At the end, both have identical R(t_0), but T_A(t_0) \gg T_B(t_0).
Apply identical perturbation A(t) > \tau (e.g., targeted node removal at rate A).
Measure R(t) over the recovery period for both networks.
FWR prediction: Network A exhibits higher minimum R during crisis and faster return to R_0.
Falsification criterion: If no statistically significant difference in recovery trajectories is observed across an ensemble of such pairs, T(t) carries no explanatory power and reduces to a redundant variable. In that case, FWR reduces to E(t) = F(t) \cdot \sin(\varphi_W(t)) \cdot R(t) with T(t) eliminated.
This experiment is implementable with standard network simulation tools (e.g., NetworkX, igraph).
8. Discussion
The FWR model interprets existence as an event, formalized through three interacting variables. Key architectural decisions in v3.0:
Sign semantics of \sin(\varphi_W): Rather than restricting \varphi_W to [0, \pi] or taking |\sin(\varphi_W)|, the negative regime is assigned ontological content. This preserves the full wave structure and introduces the annihilative mode as a genuine ontological category — not a modeling artifact.
Deterministic \varphi_0: Treating \varphi_0 as a system-specific initial condition avoids the collapse of \langle E(t) \rangle to zero under ensemble averaging. Each system's phase history is unique and non-exchangeable with other systems.
T(t) as structural memory: The move from "relational truth" to "relational accumulation" resolves the circular epistemology of earlier versions. T(t) is a dynamical variable with a clear differential equation and a falsifiable role in recovery prediction.
\gamma as free parameter: Fixing \gamma without domain justification introduces spurious precision. The framework provides structure; domain specialists supply constants.
Relationship to AGI alignment: R(t) as an alignment target variable is developed in the companion R-Aligner paper. FWR provides the ontological foundation; R-Aligner develops the engineering application.
9. Conclusion
FWR Meta-Ontology v3.0 redefines existence as a dynamic event arising from the interaction of Flow, Wave, and Resonance:
The framework is dimensionally consistent, structurally minimal, and now carries a falsifiable empirical prediction. Crisis is formalized as power-law collapse of R(t) with domain-specific \gamma. Relational accumulation T(t) captures structural memory and generates differential predictions under identical instantaneous conditions. The sign of \sin(\varphi_W) distinguishes constructive and annihilative modes of existence.
10. Future Work

Empirical case study: Identify a real-world dataset (organizational disruption, online community collapse) in which FWR variables can be operationalized as predictive rather than descriptive.

Biological aging: Model T(t) accumulation and R(t) decline as structural analogs of biological aging; test whether interventions that restore R(t) also restore T(t) growth rate.
State-dependent forgetting: Explore \lambda = \lambda(F(t)) — forgetting rate as a function of flow intensity — as an extension of T_\lambda(t).
Annihilative regime dynamics: Characterize systems that persist in E(t) < 0 for extended periods; determine whether the Dark Phase and the annihilative regime are co-extensive or distinct.
