Abstract
This study proposes the FWR Meta-Ontology, which defines existence not as a fixed substance but as a dynamic event emerging from the interaction of Flow, Wave, and Resonance. The model conceptualizes existence as a time-dependent structure, where the manifestation of being is determined by variations in relational connectivity and phase alignment.

The state of existence is formalized as:

E(t) = F(t) · sin(φ_W(t)) · R(t)

where F(t) is the magnitude of the rate of state change, φ_W(t) is the accumulated phase generated by temporal integration of instantaneous wave intensity W(t), and R(t) is a dimensionless order parameter representing relational connectivity density.

When external perturbation A(t) exceeds a critical threshold τ, the resonance network undergoes a power-law collapse governed by a domain-specific exponent γ. This collapse defines the Dark Phase: a metastable regime preceding structural reconfiguration, characterized by reduced relational connectivity but latent potential for new relational formations.

Relational accumulation T(t) = ∫R(τ)dτ is introduced as a measure of the historical depth of a system's relational structure. T(t) formalizes why existence persists over time, capturing the structural memory that E(t) alone cannot express.



1. Introduction
Traditional ontology has tended to regard existence as a fixed entity. This paper argues that existence should instead be understood as a relational and temporal process of emergence. Existence is not a self-contained object but an event continuously constituted through interaction and transition.

This study proposes the following core proposition:

Existence is a dynamic event emerging from the interaction of Flow, Wave, and Resonance.

This proposition avoids reduction to a single ontological cause while providing a unified framework to explain the generation, dissolution, stability, and collapse of existence through dynamical structure.



2. Dynamical Formulation of Existence
2.1 Existence Equation
E(t) = F(t) · sin(φ_W(t)) · R(t)

Existence cannot be reduced to a single ontological term. It emerges as a composite phenomenon of flow, phase, and relation. The sin(φ_W) term reflects that existence becomes manifest through phase alignment, consistent with general properties observed in oscillatory systems, where phase synchronization maximizes observable states.

2.2 Component Definitions
(1) Flow

F(t) = |dS(t)/dt|

S(t) denotes the system state; F(t) is the magnitude of its rate of change. Flow captures the system's dynamism, transformability, and energetic drive.

(2) Wave

φ_W(t) = 2π ∫₀ᵗ W(τ) dτ + φ₀

W(t) = (1/2π) · dφ_W(t)/dt

W(t) is the instantaneous wave intensity; φ_W(t) is the accumulated phase. Wave influences existence indirectly through phase structure rather than amplitude alone.

(3) Resonance

R(t) is a dimensionless order parameter satisfying 0 ≤ R(t) ≤ 1, representing relational connectivity density. Resonance is not an intrinsic property of isolated entities but an emergent property of relationships within the system.

It admits two concrete specifications:

Network-based:   R(t) = (1 / N(N-1)) × Σ_{i≠j} A_{ij}(t)

Information-based:   R(t) = I_shared(t) / I_total(t)

The choice of specification is domain-dependent and does not alter the structure of E(t).



3. Crisis Axiom and Dark Phase
3.1 Crisis Condition
A(t) > τ

A(t) denotes external perturbation intensity, defined operationally as the rate of node removal or connection loss per unit time within the relational network. τ is the critical threshold below which the system maintains normal resonance.

3.2 Piecewise Resonance Model
Resonance under crisis is modeled as:

R(t) = R₀                        if A(t) ≤ τ  [normal state]

R(t) = R₀ · (τ / A(t))^γ       if A(t) > τ  [crisis state]

This describes power-law decay of resonance density beyond the critical threshold. The exponent γ > 0 is a free parameter, not fixed a priori. Its value depends on the network topology of the domain under study and must be determined empirically or derived from domain-specific percolation theory.

Note: γ ≈ 2.5 in 2D lattice percolation (Stauffer & Aharony, 1994), but FWR makes no assumption that relational networks share 2D lattice topology. Domain-specific calibration is required.

The full existence equation under crisis thus reduces to:

E(t) = F(t) · sin(φ_W(t)) · R₀ · (τ / A(t))^γ

retaining dimensional consistency throughout, as R(t) remains dimensionless.

3.3 Dark Phase
The Dark Phase is characterized by three properties:

Collapse of resonance networks (R(t) → 0)

Internal energy accumulation (F(t) may remain nonzero or increase)

Metastable pre-reconfiguration state

This phase resembles latent heat regions in thermodynamic phase transitions: the system absorbs perturbation without immediate structural change, but contains conditions for new relational formation. The Dark Phase is not mere destruction but a preparatory regime.



4. Relational Accumulation T(t)
4.1 Definition
T(t) = ∫₀ᵗ R(τ) dτ

dT/dt = R(t)

T(t) is the time-integrated relational connectivity density. It is not a definition of truth or propositional correctness; it is a structural variable capturing the historical depth of a system's relational configuration.

4.2 Interpretation
The relationship between E(t) and T(t) within FWR:

E(t) describes the instantaneous state of existence: how a system exists at moment t.

T(t) describes the accumulated relational structure: why a system persists, and how deep its relational history is.

T(t) functions as the structural memory of E(t). A system with high T(t) has built dense relational history and is more resistant to perturbation. A system with low T(t), regardless of its instantaneous E(t), has shallow structural roots.

During crisis, R(t) decreases, causing dT/dt to slow. The rate of relational accumulation falls. This captures the phenomenology of structural weakening without annihilating accumulated history: T(t) never decreases, but its growth rate is suppressed.

4.3 Exponentially Weighted Variant
For applications where distant history should carry less weight:

T_λ(t) = ∫₀ᵗ R(τ) · e^{-λ(t-τ)} dτ

The decay constant λ ≥ 0 is domain-specific. At λ = 0, T_λ(t) = T(t).

6. Stability and Transition Analysis
High-R states provide structural stability but reduced adaptability. Low-R states allow flexibility but lack structural coherence. Balanced intermediate states maximize sensitivity to perturbation and capacity for reorganization.

Existence is thus redefined as a problem of dynamic coordination among stability, complexity, and adaptability, rather than static optimization of any single variable.

F(t), φ_W(t), and R(t) jointly determine the character of existence at each moment. No single variable is sufficient. This multiplicative structure is a design commitment of FWR: the suppression of any one component to zero annihilates E(t), regardless of the magnitude of the others.



7. Discussion
The FWR model interprets existence as an event and formalizes it through three interacting variables. The crisis model reframes perturbation not as failure but as a condition for structural transition. The Dark Phase captures the phenomenology of systems under severe stress while preserving the possibility of reconfiguration.

A key architectural decision in this version: the removal of an additive crisis term from E(t). Crisis is entirely absorbed into R(t) via the piecewise definition. This preserves dimensional consistency and simplifies the core equation.

T(t) as relational accumulation resolves the earlier circularity of framing R-integration as a definition of truth. T(t) makes no epistemological claims; it is a dynamical state variable with a clear differential equation.

The exponent γ is intentionally left as a free parameter. Fixing γ without domain-specific justification would introduce spurious precision. Domain specialists or empirical calibration should determine γ for each application context. This is consistent with FWR's design principle: provide a structural framework, not pre-populated constants.

Relationship to AGI alignment: R(t) as a target variable for alignment objectives is explored in a companion paper (R-Aligner). The present paper establishes the ontological foundation; R-Aligner develops the engineering application.



8. Conclusion
FWR Meta-Ontology redefines existence as a dynamic event arising from the interaction of Flow, Wave, and Resonance. The core equation E(t) = F(t) · sin(φ_W(t)) · R(t) is dimensionally consistent and structurally minimal.

Crisis is formalized as a power-law collapse of R(t) with a domain-specific exponent γ, without introducing additive terms that would break dimensional homogeneity.

Relational accumulation T(t) = ∫R(τ)dτ captures the historical depth of relational structure, complementing the instantaneous description provided by E(t).



9. Future Work
Dynamic network simulations: validate piecewise R(t) model against synthetic network collapse data; calibrate γ for Erdos-Renyi, Barabasi-Albert, and empirical social network topologies.

Empirical analysis in socio-economic systems: formulate testable predictions using FWR variables mapped to measurable proxies; distinguish FWR-specific predictions from those derivable by existing network theory.

AGI alignment: develop R(t) as an alignment target variable in reinforcement learning architectures (see R-Aligner companion paper).

Biological aging and rejuvenation: model T(t) accumulation and R(t) decline as structural analogs of biological aging; explore interventions that restore R(t).

Empirical case study: identify a real-world dataset (e.g., online community collapse, organizational disruption) in which FWR variables can be operationalized and tested as predictive, not merely descriptive.
